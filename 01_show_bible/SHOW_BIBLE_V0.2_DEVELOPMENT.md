## 1. The show in one breath

> **One friend can't hide a single feeling. The other won't show one.**

**Mulu** is a little cloud. Every feeling he has leaks out of him as small weather: happy breezes, sad drizzle, a guilty drip. He can't keep a secret, can't play it cool and can't pretend. **Tekla** is a small armored builder who lives in the hill below his umbrella bed. She never shows what she feels. She says "I'm fine," straightens something that was already straight, and builds something enormous instead.

Everyone can see how Mulu feels. Only the audience can see how Tekla feels. Mulu reads feelings by checking the sky above someone's head, so Tekla is the one friend he can't read.

## 2. Series premise

On a small, breezy hilltop above a village, two best friends spend their days building, growing, waiting, hiding things, surprising each other and dealing with whatever the stream carries down the hill. Mulu's feelings turn into mild rain and wind, which sometimes helps, often complicates, and never solves anything by itself. Tekla's feelings come out sideways, as tidying, bowing, building and curling into a ball. In every episode something small matters a lot to one of them. Each one's way of feeling gets in the way, and they find their way back to each other: sometimes cleverly, sometimes sweetly, sometimes by accident, sometimes just laughing.

**Format:** animated comedy (animation style and method open, §22), about 5–7 minutes per episode, visual-first with a very small spoken vocabulary (§14). Two leads, a compact reusable world, and a few wordless recurring non-characters (§19).

## 3. Viewer promise

**"You'll always know something they don't, and you'll get to watch them find it out."**

The child is the expert in the room. By episode three a four-year-old can read Mulu's weather ("the drip means he's got a secret!"). By episode ten they can read Tekla's tells ("she's straightening. She is NOT fine!"). The show never explains these codes out loud. Children learn them by watching, the way they learn to read a sibling's face. That reading game is the show's hook, its main comedy engine and its rewatch engine all at once.

Secondary promises:

- **Funny first.** Every episode has a clear physical-comedy peak.
- **Warm always.** Nobody is cruel and nobody is humiliated. Every episode ends complete.
- **Surprising.** Endings rotate (§16). You can't predict how an episode lands from its first minute.

## 4. Target audience

- **Core:** ages 4–6 (`DEC-0013`). Accessibility envelope roughly 3–7.
- **Youngest viewers (3–4)** follow the physical comedy, the weather code and the rituals.
- **Core viewers (4–6)** get the dramatic irony: they know what the characters don't.
- **Older edge (6–7)** gets the social comedy (dignity, pride, politeness stalemates) and the background running gags.
- **Co-viewing adults** get Tekla. Every adult has said "I'm fine" while straightening a cushion. Adult appeal comes from recognition, not from jokes over the child's head.
- **International:** no text in the world, a tiny translatable vocabulary, and feelings shown through physical behavior. The code is visual, not linguistic.

## 5. Tone

A sunny afternoon with a chance of drizzle. Cozy but not sleepy, silly but not frantic. Think of a funny friend with a warm heart who never lectures.

- **Tone words:** warm, bright, playful, dry, tender, a little absurd.
- **Tonal range per episode:** from slapstick to one quiet moment. Most episodes touch both ends.
- **Never:** scary, sarcastic, mean, preachy, sugary, manic.
- **Emotional intensity:** real feelings at gentle size. Sadness is a drizzle, never a storm.

## 6. Entertainment philosophy

1. **Entertainment leads; meaning follows.** Episodes are about a chair, a kite or a secret, never "about sharing." If a child comes away having seen what a hidden feeling looks like, that happened because the story was funny and true, not because anyone said it.
2. **Funny because of who they are.** The test for every gag: *would it still be funny with a different pair?* If yes, rework it. We want "Of course Tekla would do that."
3. **Relationship before powers.** An episode must work even if no weather happens. When weather does happen, it should feel like a face pulling an expression, not like a tool being used.
4. **Show the feeling before the character admits it.** This is the signature beat (§15, rule 1).
5. **Complete endings, varied shapes.** No cliffhangers. No moral spoken aloud. Some episodes end in understanding, some in a clever fix, some in a gag, some in cheerful collapse.
6. **Rewatch is earned, never forced.** Details reward attention. Nothing withholds satisfaction to force the next click.

## 7. Main characters

### 7.1 Mulu — the cloud who can't hide anything

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Is** | A small, round-topped, flat-bottomed cloud about the size of a beach ball. Floats at friend-height. Young, about "five" in behavior. Proposed: boy (owner decision, §25). |
| **In one line** | An open book who wishes, just once, he could keep a page to himself. |
| **Wants** | To be cool: to keep a secret, play it calm, handle something *without it showing*. Underneath, he wants to be a good friend and not be "too much." |
| **Fears** | That his feelings are a nuisance. His rain spoils picnics and his gusts knock things over, and someday people will get tired of the weather. |
| **Contradiction** | The harder he hides a feeling, the more it shows, and in stranger ways (§12.4). He's the most honest creature on the hill and embarrassed about it. What everyone loves about him is exactly what he's trying to fix. |
| **Weakness** | Can't wait, can't keep secrets, can't aim when excited, hovers too close when worried. Over-helps. |
| **Good at (for real)** | Gardening. When he's calm his rain is perfectly aimed, and the garden is the best on the hill because of him. Making friends with any visitor. Seeing far (he floats up to the Lookout branch). Cheering people up. Games of pretend. Honesty that is braver than he knows. |
| **Misunderstands** | Thinks no weather means no feelings. He checks the sky above Tekla's head to see how she is (the **Sky Check**), sees nothing and decides "Sunny!" He also thinks secrets are bad, because his always leak, and thinks bigger help is better help. |
| **Physical behavior** | Always slightly moving: an idle bob. Drifts toward what he loves. Turns his whole body to look, since he has no neck. Does a happy spin. Drifts backward when embarrassed. When he tries to hold perfectly still he visibly vibrates. |
| **Comic behavior** | Holds his breath to hide a feeling and puffs up, and the feeling leaks out somewhere else. Pretends to sit, lie down or be normal. Answers questions he wasn't asked because his face already answered. Overcommits to helping. |
| **Vulnerability** | When he thinks he's a burden he floats off alone to drizzle where nobody can see. That is always the wrong move, and a very sympathetic one. |
| **Why he needs Tekla** | **Rain rolls off her shell.** She is the one friend his feelings can't hurt, and she's too solid for his gusts to blow away. She steadies him, helps him aim, and never, ever minds the weather. |

### 7.2 Tekla — the builder who won't show anything

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Is** | A small armored ground creature (armadillo-inspired, final species/design open, §25) with a banded shell, a tool apron with one big pocket, and quick precise feet. Young, about "five" in behavior. Lives in a round-doored burrow in the hillside. Proposed: girl (owner decision, §25). |
| **In one line** | A closed book who secretly hopes someone will read her anyway. |
| **Wants** | To be capable and taken seriously, the one who has it handled. Underneath, she wants to be close to someone without having to be perfect first. |
| **Fears** | Looking silly. Needing help. Being seen having a feeling she can't fix. |
| **Contradiction** | She wants to be known but hides everything. **She builds what she can't say**: a chair when she wants company, a ramp when she feels left out, a fence when she's hurt. Her builds are feelings in wood and string, and she's the only one who doesn't know it. |
| **Weakness** | Pride. Won't admit a mistake: she commits to it instead ("Meant to do that"). Overbuilds. Treats feelings as problems to fix with tools, plans and umbrellas. Over-rules games. |
| **Good at (for real)** | Building and fixing. Digging. Finding lost things, because she knows where everything is. Keeping secrets perfectly. Staying calm in a real mess, which matters when Mulu is spinning. Rolling fast. Strong for her size. Patient. |
| **Misunderstands** | Thinks people like her because she's useful. Thinks laughing at yourself is losing. Thinks every feeling needs a fix, so she brings a bucket to a sad moment. |
| **Physical behavior** | Quick small steps, then a dead stop. Very upright posture with arms folded. Economical gestures and a mostly composed face. Stillness is her acting. |
| **Tells (the audience learns these)** | **The Straightening:** tidies things that are already tidy when she's hurt or upset. **The Build:** starts a big project when a feeling is too big to say. **Tap-tap:** her claws tap a rhythm when she's nervous (a sound cue). **The Curl:** becomes a perfect ball when overwhelmed, whether happy or sad. **The Swish:** a small tail swish that means she's truly happy. It's rare, and kids learn to treasure it. |
| **Comic behavior** | "I'm fine." (She is not.) The **Tiny Bow** after a mishap, as if it were a performance. Deadpan commitment to a bad plan. Measuring things that can't be measured, like Mulu. Labeling everything with little picture tags. |
| **Vulnerability** | Lonely inside the shell. When she feels unwanted she doesn't say so. She works harder until the hill is spotless and she's alone on the bench. |
| **Why she needs Mulu** | He feels things out loud, so she doesn't have to be the brave one about feelings. He makes her laugh her real laugh, which nobody else gets. His weather makes her tidy world alive. And little by little, he learns to read her. |

### 7.3 The twelve-state check

Both leads can be right, wrong, confident, uncertain, selfish, generous, afraid, brave, playful, frustrated, inventive and mistaken. None of these states breaks the characters, and no competence hierarchy results:

- **Mulu is right** when Tekla's plan ignores a feeling. **Tekla is wrong** when her machine misfires or her pride snowballs a small mistake.
- **Mulu is the competent one** in the garden, with visitors and from the Lookout. **Tekla is the chaotic one** when she overbuilds or commits to "meant to do that."
- **Mulu is selfish** when he wants the praise for helping. **Tekla is selfish** when she hoards the project.
- **Mulu is brave** about feelings. **Tekla is brave** about everything else. They are brave in different places, and that is the whole relationship in four words.

## 8. The relationship

**They are best friends, neighbors and near-opposites who fit together like an umbrella and rain.**

- **The core image:** Mulu floating low over Tekla while she's curled into a ball. It's the **Cloud Hat**, the pair's comfort pose. It's also the show's logo idea (§21).
- **The core fact:** rain rolls off her shell. His feelings can't hurt her, and her silence can't fool him forever.
- **The core gap:** he shows everything and reads nothing without weather; she shows nothing and reads him perfectly. The audience reads both.
- **Status:** peers. Neither is the grown-up. Both lead, both mess up, both repair. Who starts the trouble rotates (§16.2).
- **Affection style:** they rarely say it. Mulu shows it with weather and hovering. Tekla shows it with builds, tea and keeping his secrets. Once in a long while one of them says it in three words or fewer, and it lands.

## 9. Emotional dynamic

What pulls them apart:

- Mulu's weather disrupts Tekla's tidy world: gusts scatter her rows and drizzle soaks her picnic.
- Tekla's composure makes Mulu feel his feelings are "too much" by comparison.
- Mulu misreads Tekla's silence as "fine," and Tekla lets him.

What pulls them back:

- Tekla is the only one who is never bothered by his weather.
- Mulu is the only one who eventually notices her tells.
- Each is secretly generous in their own language: his is weather, hers is building and secret-keeping.

**Across a season (light arc, never required to follow an episode):** Mulu slowly learns to look at Tekla's hands instead of the sky above her head. Tekla slowly lets one true feeling out loud. The season-closing image is Tekla drawing a symbol in *her own* square on the Forecast Board (§18), which has been empty all season. Episodes mostly reset; these small arc steps are the reward for loyal viewers.

## 10. The world: the Hill

A compact playground for comedy. There's no mythology to learn: one hill, one stream, one garden, one village you almost never see.

- **Where:** a small, breezy hilltop above a village. The village shows only as distant rooftops and the path down.
- **Who lives here:** Mulu and Tekla. Grown-ups exist in the village and stay offscreen (§19).
- **Physical logic:** gentle, consistent cartoon physics. Things fall, roll, float and dry predictably, so children can anticipate them.
- **Time:** most episodes take place in one day. Seasons change across the series, which gives visual variety through color and props without new sets.
- **Text-free world:** signs, labels and letters use pictures only. Pre-readers can follow everything, and nothing needs translating.

### What the world keeps handing them (story generators)

- **The stream brings things.** Paper boats, a lost boot, a mysterious key, a drawing from Somebody Downstream.
- **The garden needs things.** Water, patience, protection, harvest.
- **The Snail is going somewhere.** Slowly. All season.
- **The village asks for things.** Muffins for the stall, a borrowed ladder to return. Requests arrive as picture-notes on the post and notice board.
- **The sky does its own thing.** Real weather — real rain, real wind, pollen, autumn leaves — is bigger than Mulu and never his fault, even when it looks like it.
- **Things break, grow, arrive and change.**

**Height rule:** Mulu can float up to the Lookout branch but not into the high sky, where the Big Clouds live. This keeps the camera, the scale and the stories grounded.

## 12. Weather and expressive rules

Mild rain and mild wind are the entire power set (`DEC-0016`). Nothing here adds a power. These rules define *how* the two effects express Mulu. **Each rule below is proposed for owner ruling.** Rules 1 and 2 answer the open trigger-scope question from Stress Test 001 (`RSK-0004`, NF-3).

### 12.1 The eight rules

1. **Feelings leak; bodies don't.** Only feelings and intentions make weather. Sneezes, hiccups, burps and yawns are ordinary sneezes, hiccups, burps and yawns. There are no bodily-function weather gags. A dream is a feeling, so a rare sleep-drizzle is allowed. A sigh is a feeling, so a sad sigh is a small breeze.
2. **Inside is acting; outside is weather.** Changes *to his body* are acting, not powers: puffiness, a pink or grey tint, drooping, smooth or ruffled edges, squash and stretch. The only things that *leave* his body and touch the world are rain (from his flat base) and wind. There is no fog, no lightning, no snow, no shape-shifting, no growing.
3. **Calm aims; big feelings spray.** When Mulu is calm and caring he can put exactly five drops on exactly one seedling. When he's excited, upset or trying too hard, the same weather comes out unaimed. Intensity always stays mild; only control changes.
4. **It always shows.** Mulu cannot suppress a feeling. Hiding it changes the *form*, never the fact. A held-in drip comes out his side. A held-in giggle squeaks out as a tiny puff. Holding his breath makes him inflate until something gives.
5. **Rain costs water; wind costs puff.** Rain uses him up. He gets visibly thinner and must refill by drinking; tea on the bench is his refill ritual. Big gusting leaves him "out of puff," wheezy and small-voiced, until he rests. Capacity has three visible states: full, normal and wisp-thin.
6. **His weather is small and follows him; the sky's weather is big and everywhere.** His rain falls only from under his own body. Real rain falls from the whole sky. A child can always tell whose rain it is.
7. **Weather never fixes a feeling.** Rain and wind can help with a task: watering, drying, carrying a paper boat, lifting a kite. They cannot resolve the story. The resolution always comes from what the characters do, understand or give up.
8. **Rain rolls off her shell.** Tekla never gets wet. Drops bead and roll off. This is both the emotional heart of the show and a major production saving (§22).

### 12.2 The weather code (what children learn)

| Feeling | What leaves his body | Body acting (not power) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Happy, playful | warm little breezes; the happy spin | bright, puffy, bobbing |
| Excited | skittery gusts he can't aim | jittery bob, puffed up |
| Sad | drizzle straight down from his base | grey tint, droops lower |
| Keeping a secret / guilty / worried | **the Secret Drip:** one repeating drop from one spot | glancing, hovering too close |
| Embarrassed | a few fat drops ("plip… plip") | pink tint, drifts backward |
| Frustrated | one sharp **huff** of wind | ruffled edges |
| Scared | **nothing at all.** He holds everything in, and the eerie stillness is the tell | tucks up small, clings near Tekla |
| Calm, caring, concentrating | gentle rain or breeze, perfectly aimed | smooth edges, steady height |

Year one should teach the first five rows thoroughly before leaning on the rest. The code must never contradict itself; the Visual Bible must make every row distinct at thumbnail size.

### 12.3 Bounds (mild means mild)

- **Rain:** at most a watering-can drizzle, only under his body, lasting as long as the feeling. Never a storm, never lightning, never flooding.
- **Wind:** at most a kite breeze. It can lift paper, leaves, ribbons, a hat or a kite. It can slowly roll a curled-up Tekla on flat ground. It cannot knock anyone over, break anything, or blow anyone away.
- **Never a weapon:** Mulu never uses weather to hurt, scare or bully. Playful drizzle games with Tekla are allowed, because she's waterproof and she decides to play.
- **Never the ending:** see Rule 7.

### 12.4 How weather creates comedy without becoming the plot

- **Forecast irony:** we see the drip before Mulu admits the secret.
- **Suppression backfire:** his hiding makes it worse and stranger. The escalation changes in *kind*, not size: drip, then side-drip, then inflating, then squeaking out.
- **Wrong weather at the wrong time:** joy gusts at a quiet moment, embarrassed plips in front of the Heron.
- **Right weather, wrong place:** perfect aim on the wrong target.
- **Absence as meaning:** when Mulu is scared, or when he chooses stillness out of love, *no* weather is the loudest signal in the episode.

## 13. The comedy engine

The show is funny for seven reasons. Every episode uses at least three, and **at least one of the first two** (§15, rule 1).

1. **"We can see it coming"** (forecast irony). Mulu's weather tells the audience first. Kids yell at the screen.
2. **"She's not fine"** (cover irony). Tekla's words say one thing and her tells say another. The Straightening escalates until the whole hill is in straight rows.
3. **"Hiding makes it worse"** (suppression backfire). This works for both of them: Mulu's held-in feelings leak stranger, and Tekla's held-in feelings make her builds bigger.
4. **"Meant to do that"** (dignity rescue). Tekla reframes a disaster as intentional and then has to *keep* doing it to prove it. Commitment escalates.
5. **"Too much help, too much plan"** (overdoing it). Mulu over-helps and Tekla overbuilds. Sometimes both at once, in opposite directions.
6. **"After you — no, after you"** (kindness collision). Both secretly give up the same thing for each other at the same moment, which produces a stalemate of generosity.
7. **"The world has opinions"** (the responsive world). The Heron's slow judging look, the Snail's perfect timing, the wind chimes that ring when Mulu arrives, the stream that delivers exactly the wrong thing.

**Toolkit on top:** anticipation and the reaction hold, visual misunderstanding, role reversal (Tekla must be the silly one; Mulu must keep a secret), repetition with variation (a rule of three that breaks on the third), callbacks, floating-vs-rolling physical comedy, and trying too hard.

**Comedy rules:**

- **Both are the butt of the joke, in rotation.** Across any five episodes, neither lead takes most of the falls.
- **Laugh with, never at.** Embarrassment is gentle and always recovered from. Nobody humiliates the other.
- **Dignity is funny; cruelty isn't.** Tekla's pride is a comic engine, never a target for punishment.
- **Pain stops at "clonk."** A pebble bouncing off Tekla's shell is the upper limit of physical harm.
- **Words are for jokes and feelings, never for explaining.** (§14)

### Recurring bits (the show's "catchphrases," mostly physical)

- **The Sky Check:** Mulu looks above Tekla's head for weather, sees none: "Sunny!"
- **"I'm fine."** Tekla, while very much not fine.
- **The Tiny Bow + "Meant to do that."**
- **The Secret Drip:** the plink of a single drop means Mulu's hiding something.
- **Vibrating stillness:** Mulu trying to be calm.
- **The Curl and the Cloud Hat:** Tekla becomes a ball; Mulu settles on top.
- **The Roll Exit:** an embarrassed Tekla curls and rolls offscreen at speed.
- **Tea refill:** Mulu drinking a whole teapot through a straw and visibly plumping up.
- **The chimes commentary:** the door chimes ring Mulu's mood before he's even in shot.

## 14. Dialogue approach

**Visual-first with a tiny spoken vocabulary** (`DEC-0013`: sparse speech where it helps comprehension; silence is not mandatory).

- **Sound-off test:** every episode's story must be followable with the sound off. Words add jokes and warmth; they never carry plot information the pictures don't also show.
- **Word budget:** about 30–60 spoken words per episode. Lines are one to four words long.
- **Signature lines** (translated into each dub language):
  - Tekla: "I'm fine." · "Meant to do that." · "Plan." · "Hmm." · "Mulu!"
  - Mulu: "Uh-oh." · "Sorry!" · "Again!" · "Look!" · "Sunny!" · "Tekla!"
- **Vocal performance carries the rest:** hums, giggles, gasps, sighs, tiny wordless reactions.
- **Recurring non-characters don't speak.** The Heron, the Snail, visitors and Somebody Downstream communicate through looks, actions and drawings.
- **No narrator.** The audience's own reading is the narration.
- **Why this works internationally:** "I'm fine" carries the same irony in any language. Dubbing is cheap, and the jokes are in the behavior.

Open for testing: whether a slightly larger vocabulary (60–100 words) improves comprehension for 4-year-olds without hurting the visual comedy (see the silent-vs-anchored comparison in the [v0.1 proposal §16](SHOW_BIBLE_V0.1_PROPOSAL.md)).

## 15. Episode rules (non-negotiables for writers)

1. **One signature beat per episode.** At least one moment where the audience reads a feeling, through Mulu's weather or Tekla's tells, before a character acknowledges it. This is the ownable engine; generic trait comedy alone doesn't qualify.
2. **The episode must work with the weather turned off.** Weather should raise the stakes and the comedy, not be the only reason the story exists.
3. **Weather never resolves the story** (Rule 7).
4. **Someone wants something small very much.**
5. **Escalation changes kind, not just size.**
6. **One physical-comedy peak** you could describe to a friend in one sentence.
7. **One quiet moment:** a held beat, even in the silliest episode.
8. **A complete ending with a button:** a final joke, image or callback.
9. **No spoken lesson.** If anyone summarizes what they learned, cut the line.
10. **Plant everything fair.** Any twist must be visible earlier on screen.

## 16. Story engine

### 16.1 Story doors (where episodes come from)

| Door | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Something to make** | Tekla builds Mulu a chair. Clouds can't sit. |
| **Something to hide** | Mulu must keep a surprise. Secrets make him drip. |
| **Something to care for** | A fledgling needs to learn to fly; the garden needs rain Mulu doesn't have. |
| **Something arrives** | The stream delivers a mysterious key. |
| **Something to wait for** | The mystery seeds; the Snail's birthday party, received at snail speed. |
| **Something to prove** | Tekla falls in the stream in front of the Heron, meant to do that. |
| **Something between them** | Left out, jealous, competing for the one sunny spot. |
| **Something changes** | Real rain for three days; the first autumn wind; a broken routine. |

### 16.2 Who drives: rotate it

In any run of ten episodes, aim for about 3–4 driven by Tekla's want or flaw, 3–4 by Mulu's, 2 by both at once, and 1–2 by the world (the stream, the sky, the village). Nobody is the permanent cause of trouble.

### 16.3 Episode shapes (avoid the single template)

- **Farce:** one lie or attempt snowballs (the chair; "meant to do that").
- **Double secret:** both are hiding something from each other (the surprise; two gifts, one plank).
- **Mystery:** something arrives and must be figured out.
- **Waiting story:** the comedy of impatience (seeds, the Snail's birthday).
- **Contest:** polite competition that escalates (the tidiest side, the quiet game).
- **Swap:** role reversal (copy day; Tekla must be the fun one).
- **Cozy day:** a small, low-stakes day with texture (the rainy day indoors).
- **Care story:** something small depends on them (the fledgling, the garden).
- **Edge adventure (showcase):** the far side of the hill, a night-time lantern send-off.

**Banned as a default:** "Mulu's weather causes a mishap → chaos → repair." It may appear occasionally as one shape among many, never as the house style. Stress Test 001 showed that without this rule the concept falls straight into that formula (baseline 75% formula; `RES-0007`).

### 16.4 Resolution menu (rotate the landing)

| Ending | What it feels like |
| --- | --- |
| **Understanding** | They see each other. A quiet, warm click. |
| **Clever fix** | A surprising solution that needs both of them. |
| **New arrangement** | A treaty, a ritual or a compromise is born, like "one shelf, forever unsorted." |
| **Funny collapse** | It all falls apart, and that *is* the ending. They laugh. |
| **The world had it** | The answer was on screen the whole time: the Snail, the stream, the planted clue. |
| **Unfinished and fine** | Not solved today, and that's okay (the Snail is still coming). |
| **Gift** | Somebody gives something that says what they couldn't. |

**Slate rules** (these answer the resolution-monoculture risk, `RSK-0004` NF-1): at most one in three episodes ends on *understanding*; any five consecutive episodes use at least three different endings; at least one in five ends on pure comedy.

## 17. Pacing and episode structure

### 17.1 Pacing

Variable and readable: neither slow TV nor a sugar rush (`DEC-0015`).

- **Hold before the payoff.** Give children time to anticipate: see the drip, see Tekla see the drip, *then* the reaction. Comedy for 4-year-olds needs about a beat longer than adult comedy.
- **Bursts, then breathers.** Physical comedy can run fast in short bursts, followed by a settle.
- **One focal action per shot.** If two things happen, stage them one after the other.
- **Quiet is allowed.** Every episode has at least one held moment with no weather and no words.
- **No strobing, no rapid-fire cutting for its own sake, no screaming.**

### 17.2 Structure (about 6:00; flexible)

| Beat | Time | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The Forecast** (cold open) | 0:00–0:25 | Morning ritual: Tekla reads Mulu's weather and draws it on the board. The symbol often hints at the episode. Title card. |
| **The Want** | 0:25–1:15 | Something arrives, starts or goes wrong; someone wants something small very much. |
| **The Try** | 1:15–2:45 | The first attempt. Character behavior (hiding, overbuilding, over-helping) creates the complication. |
| **The Snowball** | 2:45–4:15 | Escalation that changes kind. The physical-comedy peak usually lands here. |
| **The Crack** | 4:15–4:45 | The feeling shows, or the truth comes out. Often the quiet beat. |
| **The Turn** | 4:45–5:30 | A new approach, a gift, a collapse or a reveal, drawn from the resolution menu. |
| **The Button** | 5:30–5:50 | Final joke, callback or image. |
| **The Snail Check** (tag) | 5:50–6:00 | Where is the Snail now? Chimes. End. |

The Forecast and the Snail Check are *rituals*. They're short, fixed and comforting, and they don't count as story. The story in between must vary.

## 18. Rituals, motifs and rewatch design

### 18.1 Rituals

- **The Forecast Board.** Each morning Tekla draws Mulu's weather symbol (sun, drizzle, gusts, a single drop, a question mark). The board has two squares. Hers is always empty (§9).
- **Morning tea on the bench.** Mulu refills; the pair start the day.
- **The Snail Check.** Every episode ends by finding the Snail. It's on a slow, season-long journey from the bottom of the garden to the hilltop bench, where it arrives in the season finale to watch the sunset with them.
- **The Keep Shelf.** In Tekla's workshop, one small souvenir from each episode joins the shelf: a nibbled leaf, a chair leg, a paper boat. It's a visible, growing history that rewatchers can check.
- **Goodnight.** Mulu settles into his umbrella bed. His last yawn produces no weather at all (Rule 1).

### 18.2 Sound and music motifs

- **Mulu's motif:** airy and breathy, like a whistle, ocarina or soft recorder.
- **Tekla's motif:** wooden and plucked, like marimba, pizzicato or a woodblock "tap-tap."
- **The theme** braids the two.
- **Signature stings:** the Secret Drip "plink" (one bright note), the "I'm fine" sting (two dry notes), the happy-spin swirl, the chimes chord.
- **The wind chimes by Tekla's door** ring differently for each of Mulu's moods, so children can hear his feeling before they see him. This is cheap to produce and hugely expressive (§22).

### 18.3 Rewatch principles

- **First viewing:** a clear goal, big laughs, a satisfying end.
- **Second viewing:** notice the code. The drip was there before the confession; Tekla straightened the teacups in minute one.
- **Fifth viewing:** background stories. Where the Snail is, what's new on the Keep Shelf, what the Forecast symbol foreshadowed, Somebody Downstream's drawings, a chair leg from three episodes ago.
- **Fair play:** every reveal is planted on screen, and every planted clue pays off.
- **Anticipation beats surprise:** children rewatch to *see it coming*, so design moments that are better when you know they're coming.
- **No manipulative loops:** complete endings, no cliffhangers, no withheld payoff, no "watch next" bait.

## 19. Supporting-character philosophy

**No third lead.** The two friends are the show. Everything else provides a story *function*: care, social pressure or friction. None of them talks, and none of them solves anything.

| Non-character | Function | Rules |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The Snail** | Care-receiver; season-long background journey; comic timing | Never speaks, never drives a plot, never solves. Moves only forward, only slowly. |
| **The Garden** | Something to care for; Mulu's competence | It's a place, not a character. |
| **Somebody Downstream** | Implied community; exchanges paper boats and pictogram drawings | Never seen (owner decision whether they're ever revealed, §25). Can ask; never solves. |
| **The Village** | Requests and deadlines via the post and notice board; distant rooftops | Grown-ups stay offscreen. The village can ask; never solves. |
| **The Heron** | Social pressure: a patient, dignified watcher at the stream whose slow look is judgment | Wordless. Appears in at most 1 in 4 episodes. |
| **The Big Clouds** | Real weather; what Mulu is *not*; sky scale | Faceless. They're weather, not characters. |
| **Visitors** (a fledgling finch, a lost duckling, a beetle) | A one-episode care or friction function | Wordless, one per episode at most, in no more than about 1 in 4 episodes. Never resolves the story. |

**Guardrail:** if any non-character starts driving plots or needing lines, it has become a hidden lead. Cut it back.

## 20. What stories belong, and what don't

**Belong:**

- Small everyday stakes that feel huge at five: a present, a chair, a turn, a secret, a snail's birthday.
- Making, caring, waiting, hiding, sharing, surprising, trying something new, noticing.
- Real feelings at gentle size: left out, embarrassed, proud, jealous, worried, overjoyed.
- Physical comedy with floating, rolling, building and falling apart.
- Fair mysteries, polite contests, kindness collisions.
- Episodes where nothing much happens beautifully.

**Don't belong:**

- Weather as disaster, peril or severe storms; any lightning.
- Powers that fix everything, or new powers introduced to make a plot work.
- One character as the permanent fool, or the permanent grown-up.
- Spoken lessons, morals and summaries.
- Villains, bullies, fear-based stakes, danger to life or limb.
- Stories that need reading, counting or a narrator to understand.
- Bodily-function weather gags (Rule 1).
- A visitor or grown-up solving the problem.
- Adult romance, identity debates, politics, religion or ideology (`AGENTS.md` editorial gate; any such proposal is marked `SENSITIVE_CONTENT_REVIEW_REQUIRED` and escalated to the owner).
- Imitable danger: climbing high, jumping into water, rolling down steep slopes.
- Merchandise-driven plots and brand placements.

## 21. Brand and IP anchors (for later phases)

Recognizable, ownable, simple:

- **The pair silhouette:** a flat-bottomed cloud floating over a banded dome. Even more iconic is the Cloud Hat (a cloud resting on a curled ball).
- **Signature props:** the umbrella bed, the Forecast Board and its symbols, the wind chimes, Tekla's apron, the Snail.
- **Signature lines:** "I'm fine." · "Sunny!"
- **Signature sounds:** the Secret Drip plink, the chimes chord, the tap-tap.

Merchandise isn't a story driver, but these anchors are chosen so the property is recognizable at a glance.

## 22. Production-aware creative rules

These rules are designed so that the easy recurring scenes are cheap and the showcase scenes are worth their cost. They are written for any production method. No tool or renderer is selected (see the [benchmark brief](../06_production/PRODUCTION_BENCHMARK_BRIEF_V0.2.md)).

1. **Mulu is a soft solid, not vapor.** He has an opaque body with a clean outline and a fixed silhouette (dome top, flat base, one top tuft), like frosting or a marshmallow. This removes the hardest continuity problem the red team found (vapor-body drift) while keeping him unmistakably a cloud.
2. **Rain is discrete, stylized drops** falling straight down from his flat base. They're countable, never volumetric mist. A drip is one drop.
3. **Wind is drawn:** stylized swirl lines plus a few objects responding. **Three-thing rule:** at most three light objects react to wind in a standard shot. Showcase shots may exceed this.
4. **Rain rolls off Tekla.** She is never wet, so the lead with the most screen time carries no wet-state continuity.
5. **The hill dries fast.** Puddles and wet patches last one scene unless the story needs them, and then they're tracked explicitly in the shot manifest.
6. **Tekla's stoicism is performed through behavior** (straightening, building, curling, the bow), not micro-expressions. Big readable actions carry her hidden feelings.
7. **Home base first.** About 60% of a season happens on the Hilltop, using a small library of fixed camera setups.
8. **Designed contact only.** Two-character contact is limited to a small approved set: Cloud Hat, nub-to-paw touch, shading, and handing over a teacup. New contact types are showcase-only.
9. **Prop discipline:** five hero props per shot at most. Recurring props are built once, with fixed states (open/closed, full/empty).
10. **Sound does heavy lifting.** Chimes, stings and foley carry emotion that would be expensive to animate.
11. **Standard lighting:** morning, day and golden hour on the Hilltop are standardized setups. Night and storms are showcase-only.
12. **Episode tiers** (mirrors Stress Test 001's burden mix of about 6 MEDIUM : 3 HIGH : 1 EXTREME):
    - **Tier A, "Hilltop"** (about 6 in 10): home base, both leads, at most 4 weather moments, standard lighting.
    - **Tier B, "Out and about"** (about 3 in 10): one secondary set, moderate effects, maybe one visitor.
    - **Tier C, "Showcase"** (about 1 in 10): water, night, many objects or a big weather set piece. This is where the property shows off.

## 23. Originality principles

**Nearest neighbors (don't drift toward these):**

- **Cloudbabies** (sky caretakers).
- **Middlemost Post** (a reformed rain-cloud lead in a workplace comedy).
- **Partly Cloudy** (Pixar short): a cloud whose output hurts his loyal friend, who armors up. **Do not stage** a friend putting on protective gear to endure Mulu. Tekla's shell is her nature, not equipment.
- **Regular Show's C.J.:** a cloud character whose anger becomes storms. Our weather is mild, never stormy, and we're a preschool reading game, not a temper gag.
- **The Personal Raincloud** trope (generic).
- Picture-book clouds that want to be big or useful (*Cloudette*, *Little Cloud*), and The Marshmallow Times' eager-to-be-useful Cloud. **Do not** make "small cloud wants to be big and useful" Mulu's arc.
- **Wonder Pets' Tuck** (a shelled preschool lead), which is why "Tuck" was rejected as a name.
- **Hedgehog in the Fog:** avoid hedgehog-plus-fog imagery.

Structural dyad precedents such as *Frog and Toad* and *Pat & Mat* may be studied as abstract structure only. See the [name screen](../03_research/NAME_SCREEN_002.md) for sources.

**What we own (build and protect this layer):**

- The "can't hide / won't show" pair.
- The two-sided reading game, where the audience reads both leads.
- Rain rolling off her shell, and the Cloud Hat.
- The Sky Check.
- The Secret Drip.
- "I'm fine" with the Straightening.
- "She builds what she can't say."
- The Forecast Board with its empty second square.
- The Snail's season journey and the Keep Shelf.

**Rules:**

- Study comedy *mechanics*, never scenes, gags or designs ([originality rules](../02_story_system/ORIGINALITY_RULES.md)).
- Design from written decisions and documented divergence from neighbors, never from "cute cloud" prompts. Generic prompts converge on the average cloud.
- Every new recurring bit gets a one-line nearest-neighbor check before it becomes a series element.
- Names, title and silhouettes need a deeper screen, and counsel if the owner wishes, before any canon lock. Desk screens are not legal clearance.

## 24. How v0.2 relates to earlier development

This draft is the working synthesis of Research Sprint 001, Red-Team Review 001 and Stress Test 001. It keeps what worked and drops what felt engineered.

- **Kept and sharpened:** hook H1 (it now works in both directions, as a two-sided reading game); the expressive power philosophy (now eight plain rules); the character comedy engine E1–E4 (now seven named engines); the third-force layer (now concrete: the Snail, the stream, the village, the Heron); and both anti-formula rules (§15 rule 1, §16.4).
- **Replaced:** the planner-vs-helper split (Structure A). It kept drifting toward "responsible one + chaos machine." The new axis is **shows everything vs. shows nothing**. Each lead has an involuntary outlet (weather / building and tidying) and a blind spot, and each is brave in a different place. Mulu's core want also changed from "to be needed" to "to be able to keep something to himself," which moves him away from the eager-helper-cloud neighbors.
- **Dropped:**
  - fog as an effect (a stealth fourth power)
  - sneeze- and hiccup-gusts (bodily gags)
  - Pip's "contingency lists for feelings" (a trait corridor)
  - the old names (a desk-level pair collision, `RSK-0004`)
  - the requirement to explain Nimbus as a "helper."
- **Unchanged decisions:** ages 4–6, two leads, visual-first sparse speech, 5–7 minutes, cause-and-effect comedy, implicit learning, mild rain and mild wind only, powers never auto-solve, balanced agency (`DEC-0013`–`DEC-0016`).
