★ = recommended development pilots (Part 2).

### Why the other seven are not in the first three

- **Kite Day** is the strongest reserve and the best pure wind episode. Its central joke (you can't feel on command, so the deadpan friend tries to be fun) is excellent. Its ending shape ("the kite flies behind them unnoticed") is a familiar gag and needs a fresher button before it leads a slate.
- **Meant To Do That** is maybe the funniest concept on the list. Stream contact, splashes and the Heron make it a Tier C showcase, which is too expensive for a first test of the character engine. Keep it as the first showcase.
- **Not Me** is cheap and teaches the "his weather vs. the sky's weather" rule. It works better as a quiet episode four than as a first impression.
- **Something Came Down the Stream** is a great series engine, but in its current form it leans on the world more than on who they are.
- **Out of Puff** is a strong capacity-cost episode. It should wait until the rule is established.
- **Two Gifts, One Plank** has a lovely structure but uses no weather, and pilot 1 already tests the no-weather engine.
- **The Dream Drizzle** can't be developed until the owner rules on sleep triggers, and it's the most expensive concept here.

### Pilot 1 — "A Chair for Mulu" (the visual-comedy pilot)

**Hook.** Tekla builds her best friend a beautiful chair. There's one problem: clouds can't sit.

**Goal.** Tekla wants Mulu to sit in the chair she made. What she really wants, which she'd never say, is for him to sit *with her* at sunset. Her own old chair stands by the bench, and the new one is its twin.

**Conflict.** Physics says no. Tekla's pride won't allow a design flaw, and Mulu wants so badly to make her happy that he'll try anything.

**Escalation (each attempt changes kind):**

1. **The unveiling.** A drumroll, then the sheet-pull. Mulu gasps with joy, and a happy gust blows the sheet back over Tekla. She stands under it like a small, dignified ghost. Tiny Bow: "Meant to do that."
2. **Sit #1.** Mulu lowers himself onto the chair and hovers a finger's width above it, bobbing. He holds a proud "sitting" pose. The kids see the gap.
3. **Measuring.** Tekla measures Mulu with a tape. The tape sinks into his fluff and comes out wiggly. She saws a chair leg to adjust, and now the chair wobbles too.
4. **The seatbelt.** Mulu squeezes out through the gaps like dough and pops back up to his usual shape.
5. **Pebbles.** She loads his nubs with pebbles and he sinks slowly, triumphantly… until a butterfly passes. He's delighted, a gust lifts him, and the pebbles drop onto Tekla's shell. *Clonk.* Beat. "I'm fine." She straightens a chair that's already straight. (First signature beat.)
6. **Tea.** Mulu drinks the whole teapot, then a watering can through a straw, and gets heavy enough to settle into the chair. Victory! He's so happy that the extra water comes out as happy rain, and the seat becomes a puddle. Tekla drills a drain hole. "Plan."
7. **Upside-down.** Tekla flips her thinking and mounts the chair upside-down under the Lookout branch. Mulu floats up and "sits" in it upside-down. It works! They celebrate.

**Funniest moment.** The pebble *clonk*: a perfectly straight face, "I'm fine," and a slow, dignified straightening of her own shell bands. The runner-up is tea-bloated Mulu sitting proudly while quietly raining into his own seat.

**Emotional beat.** From his new chair up in the Lookout, Mulu sees the whole hill: Tekla walking back to the bench at sunset, sitting in her old chair next to an empty twin. He understands the chair was never about sitting.

**Weather role.** Light and expressive only: one happy gust (the sheet) and one happy rain (the overfull tea). The episode would work without either. This pilot tests the character engine, not the weather.

**Ending.** Mulu floats down. He can't sit, so he does it the Mulu way: he settles low and wide behind her old chair like a big soft backrest, and Tekla leans back into her friend. The twin chair stays empty. **Button:** the Snail, which has been inching toward the empty chair all episode, arrives, climbs onto the seat, and sits. Mulu looks at the Snail, then at Tekla: *even the Snail can sit.* Tekla's tail: one small swish. **Snail Check:** already done, the Snail is right there. Chimes. **Keep Shelf:** the sawn-off chair leg.

**Production difficulty: MEDIUM (Tier A).** Hilltop plus the Lookout Tree; two leads; hero props are the chair, tape, pebbles, teapot, straw and sheet. Watch-points:

- **Hover-gap consistency:** the gap between Mulu and the seat is the joke and must read in every shot.
- **Squash-and-stretch:** the seatbelt squeeze must return exactly to model.
- **The upside-down sitting shot.**
- **The sheet:** a single cloth simulation, which could be swapped for a stiff cover if cost bites.

**What this pilot tests.**

1. Can the show be very funny with almost no weather?
2. Does Tekla carry an episode as the driver, and does her pride read as funny rather than bossy?
3. Does "escalation by kind" hold 4–6-year-olds?
4. Do the Mulu body rules (hover, squash, return to model) survive physical comedy?
5. Is the story fully followable with the sound off?

### Pilot 2 — "Perfectly Fine" (the relationship pilot)

**Hook.** A fledgling finch discovers that Mulu's breezes are the best way to practice flying. Tekla is fine. Perfectly fine.

**Goal.** Mulu wants to help the fledgling fly. Tekla wants not to mind, or more precisely, to not be seen minding.

**Conflict.** Tekla feels left out and hides it. Mulu reads feelings by checking the sky, and there's no weather above Tekla.

**Escalation:**

1. **The Forecast.** Tekla draws a big sun for Mulu. Her own square stays empty. She has set out two teacups on the bench for their morning tea.
2. **The fledgling** flutters clumsily down from the Lookout nest, practicing. Mulu floats it gently on a soft breeze, and the fledgling loves it. Joy gusts, spins, a new best game.
3. **Tea for one.** Mulu is busy and doesn't come. Tekla: "Fine." She straightens the teacups.
4. **The Straightening montage.** Garden stakes, the pebble border, pinecones lined up by size, grass combed. Finally she gently rotates the Snail so it faces the same way as the pebbles. The kids are shouting.
5. **The Sky Check.** Mulu zooms over to share his joy. He checks above her head: clear. "Sunny!" He zooms back, and his joy gust scatters all her rows. She re-straightens, faster.
6. **The ramp.** Tekla builds a flight-training ramp for the fledgling. It's her way in: "she builds what she can't say." The fledgling prefers the breezes, and the ramp stands unused. Tekla curls into a ball, for just one second, then uncurls. "Fine."
7. **The clue trail.** Mulu notices the Snail facing the wrong way — no, the *right* way. Everything is straight. He follows the straightened things like a detective's trail: rows, cones, cups. At the end of it is Tekla on the bench, very upright, alone.

**Funniest moment.** Tekla rotating the Snail to line up with the pebbles, gently and deadpan, while the Snail's eyestalks slowly turn back.

**Emotional beat.** Mulu does the Sky Check one more time. Nothing. Then, for the first time, he looks *at her* and sees her hands straightening her own apron pocket. He gets it: the weather is inside. He tries a small sympathy drizzle, and it rolls off her shell. She almost smiles. So he does the only thing left: he floats down beside her and stays. There's no weather, no words and a long hold. It's the stillest Mulu has ever been, and the stillness is the gift. She leans, a tiny bit.

**Weather role.** Essential, but it never resolves anything:

- **Joy gusts** unknowingly wreck her rows. The weather causes friction without malice.
- **The sympathy drizzle rolling off her shell** is the show's thesis image.
- **The absence of weather** at the key moment is how the episode lands. Its most important weather moment is no weather at all.

**Ending (understanding + button).** The fledgling lands between them, then hops onto Tekla's ramp and flies off it on Mulu's breeze. Both methods, one flight, without it being a lesson: the bird simply chooses both. Evening tea at last, with two cups. **Button:** the fledgling returns and perches on Tekla's head. Mulu checks the sky above her head and finds the bird. "Sunny!" This time he's right. **Snail Check:** the Snail has turned itself back around. **Keep Shelf:** one pinecone, crooked on purpose.

**Production difficulty: MEDIUM-HIGH (Tier B).** One visitor (a small bird: flight cycles, landing on characters) and many small props whose straightened/scattered states must be tracked across shots, so a continuity manifest is required. The joy-gust scatter shots need the three-thing rule. Tekla's acting is carried by behavior, but the long still hold at the end is a timing and acting test.

**What this pilot tests.**

1. Can 4–6-year-olds read a *hidden* feeling through behavior (the Straightening) with almost no words?
2. Is the Sky Check funny, and is its payoff moving?
3. Does the jealousy / left-out family work without making either lead unlikable?
4. Does a no-weather emotional climax land?
5. Can a wordless visitor raise stakes without becoming a third lead?

### Pilot 3 — "The Secret Drip" (the weather-identity pilot)

**Hook.** Mulu has made Tekla a surprise. Secrets make him drip.

**Goal.** Mulu wants to keep Tekla's surprise hidden until sunset. It's a set of wind chimes he made from found spoons, shells and a pebble, wrapped in a big leaf.

**Conflict.** Mulu physically can't keep a secret: every secret thought makes a single repeating drip. Tekla, who reads him perfectly and keeps secrets perfectly, spots it immediately and decides to protect his surprise by pretending not to notice. Both are now hiding something, for each other.

**Escalation:**

1. **The Forecast.** Tekla steps out. Mulu is… dripping. One drop, over and over, from one spot. She draws a single drop and a question mark.
2. **The hiding.** Mulu hides the leaf-wrapped present in the workshop. There's a neat trail of drips from his umbrella bed to the workshop door. Tekla sees it, and the kids see her see it. She steps carefully over every drop like a laser beam, humming flatly, gazing at the horizon.
3. **Moving it.** Tekla is near the workshop! Mulu panics and moves the present, leaving a new trail. He tries to hold the secret in: he puffs up with cheeks full, and the drip comes out of his side instead, sideways. (Suppression backfire.)
4. **The catching umbrella.** Mulu borrows his own umbrella bed and carries it upside-down beneath himself to catch the drips. He's a cloud carrying an umbrella for himself. It fills, it gets heavy, and he sinks lower and lower through the scene.
5. **Tekla overdoes the not-noticing.** She builds an "I am not looking" screen on stakes and works behind it, then covers her eyes with her apron "to fix something" and walks slowly into Mulu's upside-down umbrella. *Bonk.* "I'm fine." Her kindness is starting to cause its own chaos.
6. **The Snail.** Snails love wet. The Snail has been following the drip trail all episode, and it reaches the hiding place. When Mulu checks, the Snail is on the present, nibbling the leaf wrapping. Worry drips speed up. Excited-panic gusts rattle everything.
7. **The confession.** At sunset, dripping heavily, sure the surprise is ruined, Mulu gives up. He leads her to it: "Surprise…" (small, sad).

**Funniest moment.** The double performance: Mulu sinking under an umbrella full of his own secret, while Tekla, blindfolded with her apron, steps with elaborate care over drips she "can't see."

**Emotional beat.** Tekla produces the biggest, worst fake-surprised face in the history of the hill: "GASP!" It's the one big feeling she ever shows, and it's fake. Mulu stops dripping in shock. She knew. She knew all day and stepped around every drop so he could have his surprise. She kept *his* secret for him, the only way a closed book can say "I love your surprise." Then she unwraps the nibbled leaf, now a lace pattern that's even prettier, and sees what he made. The real feeling gets through: the Curl. She becomes a ball out of sheer happiness. Mulu settles on top (the Cloud Hat).

**Weather role.** Essential; it *is* the story.

- **The drip is the secret made visible.** Worry changes its form: side-drip, then speed-up.
- **Relief stops it.**
- **Happiness completes the gift:** when Tekla hangs the chimes by her door, Mulu's joy breeze rings them for the first time. That's the first "Mulu chord," the audio motif for the rest of the series. (This makes Pilot 3 the natural series opener. If another pilot airs first, the gift becomes something else and the chimes already hang by the door.)
- **The weather creates the problem and the comedy, but it never solves anything.** Kindness does.

**Ending (gift + double reveal).** The chimes ring. Mulu hears his own happiness out loud and gets more excited, so they ring more, so he gets more excited. A happy feedback loop ends with chimes spinning, Tekla's apron flapping, and her deadpan "…Fine." **Snail Check:** the Snail wears a scrap of nibbled leaf like a bow. **Keep Shelf:** the lace leaf.

**Production difficulty: HIGH (Tier B).** This is the benchmark's hardest recurring test, deliberately:

- **Discrete drips** start, stop and change rate on cue.
- **Drip trails** must stay consistent within a scene (wet-state test under the "dries in one scene" rule).
- **The umbrella prop** is carried beneath a floating character, which is a contact test.
- **Chimes motion** plus sound.
- **The Snail's position continuity.**

Tekla stays dry throughout (Rule 8), which helps.

**What this pilot tests.**

1. Is the ownable hook ("feelings you can see; a secret you can't keep") instantly clear and funny to 4–6-year-olds?
2. Do forecast irony and suppression backfire read without dialogue?
3. Does a double-secret kindness collision produce warmth without a spoken lesson?
4. Can discrete-drop rain and within-scene wet trails be produced consistently, at acceptable cost?
5. Does the audio motif (chimes) land as a series anchor?
